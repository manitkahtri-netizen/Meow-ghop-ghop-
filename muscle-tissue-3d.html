PLATE 01

Skeletal muscle

WhereAttached to bone by tendons and aponeuroses; also tongue, diaphragm, upper third of the oesophagus, extraocular muscles, muscles of facial expression, and the external anal and urethral sphincters.

The cell

The unit is the muscle fibre: long, cylindrical and unbranched, 10–100 µm across and up to several centimetres long — sartorius fibres reach roughly 30 cm.

It is a multinucleate syncytium formed by the fusion of embryonic myoblasts. Hundreds of nuclei lie flattened against the sarcolemma at the periphery. That peripheral position is the single quickest way to identify skeletal muscle down a microscope.

Wedged between the sarcolemma and the external lamina sit satellite cells — quiescent myogenic stem cells, the source of what limited regeneration this tissue has.

The sarcomere

Myofibrils run the length of the fibre in register, so their banding reads straight across the whole cell.

  • A band — full length of the thick filaments plus overlap. Dark, anisotropic.
  • I band — thin filaments only. Light, isotropic, bisected by the Z disc.
  • H zone — thick filaments only, in the centre of the A band.
  • M line — myomesin and creatine kinase, cross-linking thick filaments.
  • Z disc — α-actinin, anchoring the thin filaments.

Z disc to Z disc is one sarcomere, about 2.0–2.5 µm at rest.

Filaments

Thick — myosin II: two heavy chains folded into head, hinge and tail, plus four light chains. The ATPase sits in the head.

Thin — an F-actin double helix carrying tropomyosin and the troponin complex (TnT binds tropomyosin, TnI inhibits, TnC binds Ca²⁺).

Accessory — titin, the molecular spring running Z to M; nebulin, the thin-filament ruler; and dystrophin, which ties actin to the sarcolemma and external lamina and is the protein lost in Duchenne muscular dystrophy.

Membrane systems

T-tubules are deep sarcolemmal invaginations sitting at the A–I junction. The sarcoplasmic reticulum is extensive and ends in paired terminal cisternae.

One T-tubule flanked by two terminal cisternae is a TRIAD — and there are two triads per sarcomere. Coupling here is mechanical: the DHP receptor acts as a voltage sensor and physically gates RyR1.

Connective tissue

  • Endomysium — delicate reticular fibres around each fibre, carrying capillaries and nerve terminals.
  • Perimysium — wraps bundles of fibres into fascicles; carries the larger vessels and nerves.
  • Epimysium — dense irregular sheath around the whole muscle, continuous with the tendon.

Control

Voluntary, via somatic α-motor neurons. At the motor end plate, acetylcholine opens nicotinic receptors, producing an end-plate potential and then an action potential.

One neuron plus every fibre it supplies is a motor unit — around 1:3 in the extraocular muscles for fine control, closer to 1:1000 in gastrocnemius for gross power.

Sensation comes from muscle spindles (intrafusal fibres, reporting length) and Golgi tendon organs (reporting tension).

Behaviour

Rapid, powerful, discontinuous contraction that fatigues. The refractory period is short, so twitches summate and tetanus is possible; force is graded by recruiting more motor units and by firing them faster. Fibres do not divide — the muscle grows by hypertrophy.

PLATE 02

Cardiac muscle

WhereThe myocardium, and extending as sleeves into the walls of the great vessels entering the heart — the pulmonary veins and the venae cavae.

The cell

Cardiomyocytes are short, branched cylinders roughly 15 µm by 85–100 µm.

Each has one — occasionally two — centrally placed nuclei, surrounded by a perinuclear clear zone holding glycogen, the Golgi apparatus and lipofuscin pigment that accumulates with age.

The sarcomeric organisation is identical to skeletal muscle, so cardiac muscle is striated — but the striations are finer and less regimented.

Intercalated discs

The defining feature: step-like junctional complexes at the ends of the cells, replacing the Z disc.

  • Fascia adherens (transverse) — the major component; anchors the thin filaments of the terminal sarcomere so contractile force passes cell to cell.
  • Macula adherens / desmosomes (transverse) — bind cells together and stop them pulling apart.
  • Gap junctions (longitudinal) — connexon channels giving low-resistance ionic coupling.

The gap junctions make the myocardium a functional syncytium — coupled electrically, but still separate cells. It is not a true anatomical syncytium the way a skeletal fibre is.

Membrane systems

T-tubules are wider, more numerous, and sit at the Z disc rather than the A–I junction. The sarcoplasmic reticulum is less elaborate, with small expansions instead of true terminal cisternae.

One T-tubule plus one SR sac is a DYAD. Coupling is chemical, not mechanical: L-type Ca²⁺ influx triggers calcium-induced calcium release from RyR2 — so cardiac contraction is critically dependent on extracellular Ca²⁺.

Metabolism

Mitochondria are enormous and lie in rows between the myofibrils, occupying 30–40% of cell volume — against roughly 2% in a fast glycolytic skeletal fibre.

With abundant myoglobin, glycogen and lipid droplets and a dense capillary bed in the endomysium, the myocardium is obligately aerobic. That is why it does not fatigue — and why it infarcts when a coronary artery closes.

Rhythm and control

Involuntary and autorhythmic. The SA node sets the pace; the impulse runs to the AV node, the bundle of His, then the Purkinje fibres — modified cardiac cells that are larger and paler, glycogen-rich, with few peripheral myofibrils and fast conduction.

Nerves modulate but do not initiate: sympathetic β₁ raises rate and force, vagal M₂ slows it, and thyroxine and catecholamines act on top.

The absolute refractory period is long — about 250 ms, spanning most of contraction. Tetanus is therefore impossible, which is exactly what guarantees the heart refills between beats.

Other features

Atrial myocytes are endocrine: their granules release ANP and BNP, driving natriuresis, diuresis and vasodilation.

Force rises with end-diastolic fibre length — the Frank–Starling relationship.

Regeneration is essentially absent. Infarcted myocardium heals by fibrous scarring, and the heart answers a chronic load by hypertrophy instead.

PLATE 03

Smooth muscle

WhereWalls of hollow viscera and tubes — gut, airways from trachea to bronchioles, bladder and ureters, uterus and uterine tubes, the tunica media of blood vessels, iris and ciliary body, arrector pili, gland ducts and the splenic capsule.

The cell

Fusiform — spindle-shaped, tapering to a point at each end, packed in staggered arrays so the broad belly of one cell lies against the tapered ends of its neighbours.

Size varies enormously: about 20 µm in a small vessel, 200 µm in the gut, and up to 500 µm in the pregnant uterus.

One central, elongated nucleus. On contraction it is drawn into a characteristic corkscrew or cigar shape — visible in the model when the wave passes.

Contractile apparatus

Non-striated. There are no sarcomeres, no Z discs, no T-tubules and no troponin. Thick and thin filaments run obliquely in criss-crossing lattices, so the cell shortens with a corkscrew twist — by up to 70–80% of resting length, far more than any striated fibre.

  • Dense bodies — cytoplasmic anchors containing α-actinin; the functional equivalent of Z discs.
  • Dense plaques — membrane-bound focal adhesions transmitting force to the matrix and to adjacent cells.
  • Intermediate filaments — desmin, and vimentin in vascular smooth muscle, networking the dense bodies together.

Caveolae — flask-shaped surface pits — stand in for T-tubules. The SR is sparse, so contraction leans heavily on extracellular Ca²⁺.

How it contracts

A completely different switch from striated muscle:

  • Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin — there is no troponin.
  • Ca²⁺–calmodulin activates myosin light chain kinase.
  • MLCK phosphorylates the myosin regulatory light chain, and cross-bridge cycling begins.
  • Myosin light chain phosphatase reverses it; Rho-kinase inhibits MLCP to sensitise the cell to Ca²⁺.

In the latch state, dephosphorylated cross-bridges stay attached — sustained tone at very low ATP cost. This is why a sphincter can hold for hours and an artery can hold tone for a lifetime.

Two functional types

Single-unit (visceral). Abundant gap junctions, so the layer behaves as a syncytium; only a few cells are innervated; spontaneous pacemaker slow waves and stretch sensitivity give coarse, wave-like activity. Gut, uterus, ureter, bladder, small arteries.

Multi-unit. Few or no gap junctions; every cell is separately innervated; no spontaneous activity, so precise graded control. Iris, ciliary body, arrector pili, vas deferens, large elastic arteries, airway.

Control

Involuntary and autonomic. There is no true neuromuscular junction — axons carry varicosities that release transmitter en passant, diffusely into the extracellular space.

It also answers directly to stretch (the myogenic Bayliss response), to hormones such as oxytocin, angiotensin II and ADH, to local chemistry — NO, CO₂, H⁺, hypoxia, adenosine, histamine — and, in the gut, to the interstitial cells of Cajal that generate the slow waves.

Two things it alone can do

Secrete. Smooth muscle cells make their own external lamina plus collagen types I, III and IV, elastin and proteoglycans. Vascular smooth muscle switches between a contractile and a synthetic phenotype — the switch at the centre of atherosclerosis.

Divide. It has the best regenerative capacity of the three, growing by hyperplasia as well as hypertrophy — which is how the uterus does what it does in pregnancy.

Clinical correlation

Where the structure gives way

Each architecture fails in its own way, and the failure follows directly from the histology.

Skeletal

  • Duchenne / Becker muscular dystrophy — loss of dystrophin, so the sarcolemma tears under its own contraction.
  • Myasthenia gravis — antibodies against the nicotinic ACh receptor at the motor end plate; fatigable weakness.
  • Malignant hyperthermia — RyR1 mutation; volatile anaesthetics trigger uncontrolled SR Ca²⁺ release.
  • Rhabdomyosarcoma — the malignant tumour of skeletal muscle lineage.

Cardiac

  • Myocardial infarction — no regenerative capacity, so the territory heals as fibrous scar.
  • Troponin I and T — cardiac-specific isoforms leak into serum; the diagnostic marker of infarction.
  • Hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathy — mutations in sarcomeric proteins such as β-myosin heavy chain and myosin-binding protein C.
  • Arrhythmia — disease of the gap-junction-coupled conducting system.

Smooth

  • Leiomyoma — uterine fibroid; the commonest benign tumour in women, and a direct consequence of retained mitotic capacity.
  • Leiomyosarcoma — its malignant counterpart.
  • Asthma — bronchial smooth muscle hyperreactivity and hypertrophy.
  • Hypertension and atherosclerosis — vascular smooth muscle switching from contractile to synthetic phenotype.
